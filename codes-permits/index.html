Codes & Permits

From “is it legal?” to “here’s the page number”

This is the part I care about most. A great material only matters if people can get it approved. Here’s where the code stands, with sources, as of September 2026.

The milestone: IRC Appendix BL

In January 2022, the U.S. Hemp Building Association (USHBA) and a long list of hemp builders, designers and code experts submitted code change proposal RB316-22 to the International Code Council. The IRC committee approved it as modified in spring 2022. It was published as Appendix BL, Hemp-Lime (Hempcrete) Construction, in the 2024 International Residential Code.[1][2][3]

While the proposal was under review it was called “Appendix AY.” The published appendix is BL. (My 2022 blog post linked the strawbale appendix by mistake. This is the right one.)

What BL covers, in plain language

  • Hemp-lime as nonbearing infill in or around a structural frame.[1][3]
  • Seismic Design Categories A–C. The proposal also covers D0–D2 with an engineered design by a registered design professional, which matters in California.[3]
  • Density of 12.5–25 lb/ft³, with prescriptive R-values per inch.[1]
  • Lab testing of your mix for density and compressive strength.[3]
  • Vapor-open finishes, no membranes (with narrow exceptions), and let-in bracing.[1]
  • No fire-resistance rating in the 2024 edition. Rated assemblies were approved at committee in October 2025 for the 2027 IRC.[1]

Key point

Published is not the same as adopted

The IRC is a model code. Its appendices only apply where a state or local government specifically adopts them.[1][11] So a line in the book doesn’t make it law where you live.

In force (per USHBA, Aug 2026)

Three jurisdictions, not 49 states

USHBA counts three places where BL is in force: Austin, Texas, and Maricopa County and the City of Surprise in Arizona. Minnesota, Virginia and Washington are in progress. Washington’s rule would make BL available for local adoption starting May 2027.[1][11][12]

California & Los Angeles

What we know about California

Appendix BL is printed in the 2025 California Residential Code.[4] But California’s code says appendix provisions don’t apply unless a state agency adopts them or a local enforcing agency adopts them.[5] USHBA reports that California’s Department of Housing and Community Development proposed not to adopt Appendix BL in the 2024 triennial cycle, so it’s printed as non-mandatory.[1]

Bottom line: whether Appendix BL applies to a project in Los Angeles, or anywhere in California, depends on your jurisdiction. Check with your local building department before you design around it.

Permits without BL: it’s been done

Building codes have long included an alternative materials, design and methods provision that lets a building official approve something the code doesn’t spell out. USHBA notes it was doing this work before Appendix BL existed, and still does most of it.[1]

  • 2014 · Rolling Hills, L.A. CountyA hempcrete home extension was permitted by L.A. County as insulating infill (not structural) after a six-month review. Ryan Hayes of TerraBuilt Construction supervised the work.[6][7]
  • CalexicoThe structural engineer on a Calexico hemp-lime ADU describes getting a full permit with stamped calculations and details, and calls it California’s first permitted hemp ADU.[10]
  • May 2026 · Los AngelesHemp Homes (Harry Sopp, “Harry Hemp”) announced that Los Angeles permitted a hempcrete ADU. Hemp Homes installed the hempcrete as a subcontractor to a local general contractor. I shared their video in my groups on May 19.[8][9]

My project

An L.A. hemp-code guideline

Since 2022, my goal has been to help create “a sort of hemp-code guideline so that more people can get approved.” This is where I’m starting: questions to bring to your building department, based on what the code and the permitted projects above actually needed.

A working draft, not legal or engineering advice.

  1. Which code edition does my jurisdiction use, and has it adopted Appendix BL?[1]
  2. If not, what do you need for an alternative materials and methods request?[1]
  3. Do you require an engineered frame design for my seismic category?[3]
  4. Which lab tests will you accept for my mix’s density and compressive strength?[3]
  5. How do I show energy compliance: R-value tables or mass-wall rules?[3]
  6. Which plasters and finishes are acceptable, and what vapor-permeance data do you need?[3]
  7. What does the fire review look like for my site and assembly?[1]

Permitted a hemp project? Tell me what they asked for.

Every approval, and every denial, makes the guideline better, especially in Los Angeles County.
