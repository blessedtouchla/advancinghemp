Hempcrete 101

The wall that got me hooked

Hempcrete, also called hemp-lime, is the material that made me fall for hemp building. Here’s the short version in plain language, with a source for every technical point.

What it is

Hempcrete is made from three things: hemp hurd (the chopped woody core of the hemp stalk), a lime-based binder, and water. The wet mix goes around or between a building frame, and as it cures it becomes a lightweight, insulating wall.[1][3]

One thing to understand from the start: hempcrete is not structural. Under the building code it’s a nonbearing infill. The wood frame carries the load, and the hemp-lime insulates, adds thermal mass, and gives the plaster something to hold on to.[1][4]

Three ways it goes in

  • Cast in place: packed by hand into temporary forms in shallow layers.
  • Sprayed: blown onto a form with a machine.
  • Precast: made ahead of time as blocks or panels, then stacked or installed.

The proposal that became the code allows all three. It says forms must either let vapor through or come off within about 24 hours, so the wall can dry.[1]

A wall that breathes

The key idea behind hemp-lime is that the wall stays vapor-open. Moisture should be able to pass through the wall instead of getting trapped inside it. The code is built around that one principle:[1][4]

  • Vapor retarders and plastic membranes on the hemp-lime are generally not allowed, with narrow exceptions.
  • Plasters and finishes have to be vapor-permeable. The proposal set a minimum of 5 perms and banned polymer additives.
  • Wall bracing is let-in bracing, because solid sheathing panels would close the wall off.

USHBA’s president notes that the exceptions in these sections are where people get into trouble. Read the membrane and finish rules closely before you draw a wall detail.[4]

The numbers in the code

Hemp-lime infill has to fall between 12.5 and 25 lb/ft³ in density (200–400 kg/m³). Lighter mixes insulate better per inch:[1][4]

12.5R-2.10
15R-1.86
20R-1.54
25R-1.20

Values from the RB316-22 proposal table. USHBA confirms the R-2.10 and R-1.20 end points for the published Appendix BL.

A mix also has to prove it holds together, with lab-tested compressive strength of at least 29 psi. And a thick enough, dense enough wall can count as a mass wall for energy-code compliance.[1][4]

Why builders choose it

These are the benefits the code writers themselves list for hemp-lime.[1][3] They aren’t my own measurements. Follow the sources for the details.

Thermal comfort

Low thermal conductivity plus thermal mass helps even out indoor temperature swings, which cuts the energy needed for heating and cooling.[1]

Moisture & mold

Vapor passes through the wall instead of condensing inside it, and the lime’s alkalinity resists mold and mildew.[1]

Carbon

The hemp takes in carbon as it grows, and that carbon stays locked in the wall. The lime binder also reabsorbs CO₂ as it cures.[1]

Fire behavior

In an ASTM E84 test, hemp-lime got the lowest possible flame-spread and smoke-developed scores.[1] The 2024 appendix doesn’t give walls a fire-resistance rating. After three one-hour ASTM E119 tests in 2024–25, the IRC committee voted in October 2025 to add rated assemblies to the 2027 IRC, which still has to finish the code process.[4][5]

A long track record

Hemp-lime started in France in the mid-1980s as a way to insulate historic buildings without trapping moisture. It’s been used around the world since then.[1][4]

What it isn’t

It isn’t load-bearing, it isn’t a quick-dry material, and it isn’t forgiving of vapor-closed details. Plan for a frame, drying time and trained hands.[1][4]

Where to go next

If you’re serious about building with hemp, the next question is always: can I get it permitted where I live? That’s what I care about most.
