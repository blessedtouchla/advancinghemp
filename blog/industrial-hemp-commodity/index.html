Make Industrial Hemp a Commodity: Why Hemp Needs Public Support to Work

Corn, soybeans, wheat and cotton have a federal safety net written into law. Industrial hemp doesn’t. Here’s what commodity status means, why fiber and grain hemp need it, and how we get there.

By Victoria Imtanes · September 25, 2026 · 8 min read

I love hemp as a building material. But loving a crop doesn’t pay a farmer’s bills. If we want hempcrete, hemp insulation and hemp fiber made in America, a farmer has to be able to plant hemp without betting the farm.

My thesis is simple. Industrial hemp, the kind grown for fiber, hurd and grain (not CBD or THC), needs to be recognized as a commodity, the way corn, soybeans, wheat and cotton are. That’s the door to the kind of public support those crops already get.

The honest economics

USDA’s latest National Hemp Report says U.S. farmers harvested 21,693 acres of fiber hemp grown in the open in 2025, averaging 3,104 pounds an acre. The whole fiber crop was worth $13.5 million, down 13 percent from 2024, at an average of 21 cents a pound.[1] By my math from USDA’s totals, that’s roughly $620 per harvested acre, before a single cost.

Grain hemp earned more per pound, $1.14, but only 7,515 acres were harvested for grain nationwide.[1] Meanwhile the whole U.S. hemp crop was worth $739 million, and $574 million of that was floral hemp grown in the open, mostly for cannabinoids.[1] The part of hemp that builds houses is the smallest slice of the pie.

Reviewing the state pilot programs in 2020, USDA’s economists found that fiber buyers weren’t available in every region, that transportation costs limited profits where margins were thin, and that hemp fiber equipment was so specialized that U.S. processors had to engineer their own or import it from Europe or China.[10] University budgets they reviewed showed potentially modest returns, eroded by the cost of hauling stalks to a processor.[10]

The chicken-and-egg problem

A decortication plant separates the long bast fiber from the woody hurd we use in hempcrete. To pay off, it needs a steady supply of stalks within trucking distance. Farmers won’t plant thousands of acres without a buyer nearby, and investors won’t build a plant without the acres. So nobody goes first.

Hemp farmers also carry a compliance burden corn growers never think about: under USDA’s rules, a sample is taken within 30 days before harvest and tested for total THC, and a lot over the limit must be disposed of or remediated.[16] For a crop headed for a wall, that’s cost and risk with no upside.

What “commodity” means in farm law

Title I of the Farm Bill is the commodity title. It names the covered commodities: wheat, oats, barley, corn, grain sorghum, long and medium grain rice, pulse crops, soybeans, other oilseeds, peanuts, and (since 2018) seed cotton.[2] Farms with base acres for those crops can enroll in two safety-net programs:

  • Price Loss Coverage (PLC) pays when the national price falls below a reference price written into law. Starting with the 2025 crop, corn’s is $4.10 a bushel and soybeans’ is $10.00.[2]
  • Agriculture Risk Coverage (ARC) pays when county revenue falls below a guarantee based on recent history.[4]

A separate list of loan commodities can get marketing assistance loans, so a farmer can borrow against a stored crop instead of selling into a bad market at harvest.[3]

These programs just got bigger. The 2025 budget reconciliation law raised reference prices, lifted the ARC guarantee from 86 to 90 percent, extended both programs through 2031, and allowed up to 30 million new base acres.[4] USDA finished allocating those base acres this month. So many farms qualified that every new allocation was trimmed by 3.69 percent.[5]

Hemp is on none of these lists.[2][3] ARC and PLC pay on base acres of covered commodities, so a hemp field simply isn’t part of that safety net.

What hemp does get today

Hemp isn’t shut out of USDA entirely. Licensed growers can use:

  • Crop insurance: a pilot Multi-Peril Crop Insurance policy for fiber, grain or CBD hemp in certain counties of 27 states, plus Whole-Farm Revenue Protection nationwide.[7][8] The pilot requires at least one year of growing history and, for grain and fiber, at least 20 acres. It doesn’t cover a crop that fails quality standards.[9]
  • NAP, FSA’s Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program, where no permanent insurance policy is available.[7]
  • FSA farm loans, including operating, ownership, beginning farmer and farm storage facility loans.[7]
  • Conservation programs such as EQIP and the Conservation Stewardship Program.[7]

That’s real, and I’m grateful for it. But it’s protection against disaster, not a price floor.

Only Congress can add hemp

The covered-commodity list is written into federal law, so Congress has to change it, usually through a Farm Bill. There’s a recent precedent. After cotton was left out of ARC and PLC in 2014, the cotton sector began pushing in 2015, and Congress added “seed cotton” as a covered commodity in the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018.[6] It took about three years and an organized industry.

There’s also a smaller door. The law defines “other oilseeds” as sunflower seed, rapeseed, canola, safflower, flaxseed, mustard seed, crambe, sesame seed, “or any oilseed designated by the Secretary.”[2] Other oilseeds appear on both the covered-commodity and loan-commodity lists.[2][3] I haven’t found any USDA designation of hemp seed, so asking the Secretary to consider grain hemp is a fair request.

An honest caveat: the reference price for other oilseeds is $23.75 per hundredweight, about 24 cents a pound. Grain hemp averaged $1.14 a pound in 2025, so a PLC payment wouldn’t trigger at today’s prices.[1][2] The near-term value would be marketing loans, recognition and better data. Fiber hemp isn’t an oilseed at all, so it would need its own category, the way seed cotton got one.

First, separate industrial hemp from cannabinoid hemp

Congress won’t write a safety net for a crop it’s still fighting about. Luckily, the separation has started. The FY2026 agriculture appropriations law (P.L. 119-37), signed November 12, 2025, rewrote the federal hemp definition around a 0.3 percent total THC limit and, for the first time, defined industrial hemp: hemp grown for the stalk and fiber, or for whole grain, oil, cake, nut, hull and other non-cannabinoid seed products.[11] Most of the new definition was due to take effect November 12, 2026. A continuing resolution signed September 2, 2026 pushed most of it to December 11, 2026.[11][12][13]

The House-passed 2026 Farm Bill (H.R. 7567) builds on that. It would have growers designate whether they produce industrial hemp, let states reduce or eliminate THC testing for industrial hemp producers, create a way to end the 10-year felony ban for people who grow only industrial hemp, and have USDA accredit testing labs. The Senate committee’s June 2026 discussion draft had no hemp provisions, so the two still have to be reconciled.[14] Similar ideas were in the Industrial Hemp Act of 2023, which was introduced but never passed.[15]

What commodity status would take

  1. A clear legal line between industrial hemp and cannabinoid hemp in farm programs. That began in 2025.[11]
  2. Reliable price and acreage data. USDA already publishes an annual National Hemp Report with acres, yields and prices by use.[1] A reference price needs that track record to continue.
  3. A reference price and base acres set by Congress, like the per-bushel prices already written into law for corn and soybeans.[2]
  4. A coalition big enough to be heard. The American Farm Bureau Federation’s 2026 policy book already supports hemp production and processing, regulation “in line with other agricultural commodities,” and a permanent USDA crop insurance policy for hemp.[30] Add hemp associations, processors and builders, and that’s a real table.

Stepping stones we can win sooner

  • Marketing loans for grain hemp through the oilseed designation above.[2][3]
  • Permanent, wider crop insurance instead of a county-by-county pilot.[8][30]
  • Processing grants and loan guarantees. USDA’s Value-Added Producer Grants offer up to $50,000 for planning or $200,000 for working capital, with a 1:1 match.[21] Coexist Build in Pennsylvania used a $49,135 grant to process and test hemp blocks, and it helped them certify the block in USDA’s BioPreferred program.[22] USDA’s Business and Industry program guarantees 85 percent of rural business loans under $5 million in fiscal 2026, and equipment is an eligible use.[23] States can help too: North Dakota awarded Valley Fiber Processing $101,000 in 2022 to help scale its decortication business.[24]
  • Federal procurement. BioPreferred sets up a federal purchasing preference for qualified biobased products.[25] Every hemp building product that qualifies is one the government can choose first.
  • Research and extension. Farmer-led trials, like a SARE-funded fiber hemp variety trial in Kansas that lined growers up with a decortication plant under construction, answer the questions lenders ask.[31]
  • Building codes. A market for hurd depends on permits. The 2024 International Residential Code includes Appendix BL for hemp-lime, but USHBA counted only three places where it was in force as of August 2026.[29] See my Codes & Permits page.
  • Carbon and energy incentives, written fresh. Research finds many hempcrete mixes can store more carbon than they emit over their life, though some heavy, cement-based mixes don’t.[27] But USDA canceled its Partnerships for Climate-Smart Commodities in April 2025, ending a $5 million Lincoln University hemp project.[26] And the federal 25C credit that covered insulation ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025.[28] A biobased insulation credit would have to be a new law.

How others did it

  • The EU: hemp farmers qualify for CAP area-based direct payments if they plant certified low-THC varieties. France, Poland and Romania add coupled income support for hemp. France grows more than 60 percent of the EU’s hemp.[17]
  • France’s co-op model: La Chanvrière works with more than 750 partner farmers, processes 55 percent of French hemp, and is investing at least €15 million in a second plant.[18] That’s the chicken-and-egg problem solved by pooling supply.
  • Canada: in 2010, federal and provincial programs put more than $5.2 million behind a hemp fiber plant in Gilbert Plains, Manitoba.[19]
  • The U.S., once before: during World War II, USDA’s Commodity Credit Corporation contracted with War Hemp Industries to produce hemp seed and fiber.[20] We’ve done this before, when the country decided it mattered.

The fair objections

  • It costs taxpayers. Every farm program does. That’s why I’d start with loans, insurance and processing, which build lasting capacity.
  • The market may not be ready. In 2020 USDA’s economists wrote that it wasn’t yet known whether U.S. fiber could compete with imports over the long run.[10] Paying for acres with no buyers would just stack up bales.
  • Price supports can reward the wrong thing. Any hemp support should reward crops that actually reach a processor.
  • The THC politics are real. Keeping industrial hemp clearly separate from cannabinoid products is the only way this moves in Congress.

Where I land

Hemp built things for this country before. Give it the tools we give corn and cotton, and it can again. Ready to help? Start with my action guide, First Steps to Make It Happen.
