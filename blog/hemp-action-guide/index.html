First Steps to Make It Happen: A Hemp Action Guide

Hemp won’t become a commodity on its own. Here’s who decides, what to ask for, and steps anyone can take this month, whether you farm, build or just want better homes.

By Victoria Imtanes · September 25, 2026 · 6 min read

In my last post I argued that industrial hemp needs to be recognized as a commodity, like corn and soybeans, to get the public support that makes a crop work. This post is the how. You don’t need to be a lobbyist. You need to know who decides, what to ask for, and to keep asking.

Two tracks run side by side. The long game is commodity status in federal farm law. The near-term win is getting hemp building approved in more cities, so there’s a market for the hurd farmers grow.

Who decides what

  • Congress writes the Farm Bill, including the list of covered commodities.[30] The House passed its 2026 Farm Bill (H.R. 7567) on April 30, 2026. The Senate Agriculture Committee chairman released a discussion draft on June 23, 2026, and it had no hemp provisions.[2] The key panels are the House Committee on Agriculture and the Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition & Forestry.[3][4]
  • USDA runs the hemp production program, crop insurance, FSA loans and NAP, the hemp statistics, and Rural Development grants.[26][27][28][13] The Secretary can also designate new “other oilseeds.”[30]
  • State agriculture departments license growers under state plans. In California that’s CDFA’s Industrial Hemp Program.[29]
  • State legislatures write state hemp laws and can pass resolutions urging Congress to act.[15]
  • The International Code Council (ICC) writes the model building codes. Anyone can submit a code change or a public comment.[16]
  • States, counties and cities decide whether a code appendix like Appendix BL applies where you live.[1][17]

Step 1: Talk to Congress

Look up your House member and two senators.[5][6][7] Then check the committee rosters to see if any of them sit on an Agriculture Committee.[3][4] If they do, your voice counts double on this issue.

Timing matters. Most of the new federal hemp definition now takes effect December 11, 2026, after a September continuing resolution delayed it.[8][9] Congress will be arguing about hemp this fall, and industrial hemp can’t get lost in the THC fight.

What to ask for, in plain words:

  1. Keep industrial hemp separate from cannabinoid products, and carry the House bill’s industrial hemp provisions into the final Farm Bill: grower designation, reduced testing for industrial hemp, USDA lab accreditation, and a path past the 10-year felony ban for industrial growers.[2]
  2. Start a path to commodity status: a reference price, base acres and marketing loan eligibility for fiber and grain hemp. Even a request for USDA to study it is a start.
  3. Ask USDA to consider designating hemp seed as an “other oilseed,” which the law already allows.[30]
  4. A permanent hemp crop insurance policy in place of today’s county-by-county pilot.[27]
  5. Processing support through programs like Value-Added Producer Grants.[28]

Say you’re a constituent, keep it to one page, and follow up. A sample letter is below.

Step 2: Use public comment periods

The Farm Bill itself moves through Congress, so your input goes to your members and the committees. The rules USDA writes to carry out the law are published in the Federal Register, usually with a comment period, and you can comment at Regulations.gov.[11][12] For example, USDA’s hemp program asked for comments on its paperwork requirements in January 2026. That window closed March 30, 2026.[10]

Set up a free Federal Register search alert for “hemp” so you hear about the next one.[12] A useful comment says who you are, gives facts from your own farm or business, and makes one specific request. Assume anything you submit may be made public.

Step 3: Help USDA count hemp

Every commodity program runs on data. USDA’s National Hemp Report gives acres, yields and prices by use.[13] Congress would need that record to set any reference price. If you grow hemp, answer the survey. And ask your members to make sure USDA keeps collecting detailed fiber and grain price data.

Step 4: Go through your state and your Farm Bureau

  • State resolutions. A state legislature can formally urge Congress to act. New Jersey’s Assembly did this for the Industrial Hemp Farming Act of 2015.[15] Ask your state legislator to introduce a resolution urging Congress to recognize industrial hemp as a farm commodity.
  • Farm Bureau. The American Farm Bureau Federation’s 2026 policy book already supports regulating hemp in line with other agricultural commodities and a permanent crop insurance policy for hemp. It says more than 2,800 county Farm Bureaus write their own member-approved policies.[14] If you farm, join your county Farm Bureau and bring a hemp resolution.

Step 5: Build the coalition

These groups are active as of September 2026, and each one is a place to plug in:

  • U.S. Hemp Building Association, which led the code proposal that became Appendix BL.[21][1]
  • Hemp Building Institute, a research, education and affordable-housing nonprofit.[22]
  • National Hemp Association, which says it’s focusing on fiber and grain.[23]
  • Vote Hemp, a U.S. hemp farming advocacy group.[24]
  • International Hemp Building Association.[25]
  • Your state’s hemp growers’ association, if it has one.

A sign-on letter: I’d like to gather farmers, processors, builders, architects and homeowners behind one short letter to the Agriculture Committees. If you want your name on it, email advancinghemp@gmail.com with your name, role and state.

Step 6: Get Appendix BL adopted locally

This is the win we can get sooner. Appendix BL, the hemp-lime appendix in the 2024 International Residential Code, only applies where a state or local government adopts it.[1][17] As of August 2026, USHBA counted three places where it’s in force (Austin, Texas, and Maricopa County and the City of Surprise in Arizona), with Minnesota, Virginia and Washington in progress.[1]

  • Ask your city or county to include Appendix BL the next time it adopts a residential code. Call the building department to ask when that is, then tell your council member or supervisor.
  • Follow the ICC. Fire-rated hemp-lime assemblies were approved at the committee level in October 2025 for the 2027 IRC.[1] Anyone can take part in the ICC process.[16]
  • In California, Appendix BL is printed in the 2025 California Residential Code, but the state didn’t adopt it, so it’s non-mandatory.[18][1] Cities and counties normally adopt voluntary appendices by ordinance filed with the Building Standards Commission.[19] But a 2025 state law, AB 130, froze new state and local building standards for homes from October 1, 2025 to June 1, 2031, with narrow exceptions.[20] Local appendix adoption runs through the same code sections AB 130 changed, so a California city may not be able to newly adopt BL for homes before 2031 unless an exception applies.[19][20] Ask your building department. Meanwhile, the “alternative materials” route still works: projects have been permitted that way.[1] In Los Angeles, that conversation starts with LADBS.[32] See my Codes & Permits page for questions to bring.

Step 7: Put your money where the walls are

  • Everyone: buy and specify hemp building products, and ask suppliers where their hurd is grown. My Suppliers page is a start.
  • Farmers: line up a processor before you plant. Ask about crop insurance or NAP, and report your hemp acres to FSA as required.[26][27]
  • Builders and makers: document every permit, and look into USDA BioPreferred certification for your products.[31]
  • Homeowners: ask your architect about hemp, and show up at code adoption hearings.

Sample letter to your representative

Subject: Please support industrial hemp as a farm commodity

Dear [Representative / Senator] [Last name],

I’m a constituent in [city], and I [grow / build with / want to use] industrial hemp, the kind grown for fiber, hurd and grain, not for CBD or THC.

Industrial hemp isn’t a covered commodity under Title I of the Farm Bill, so hemp farmers have no price safety net like growers of corn, soybeans or wheat. I’m asking you to:

  1. Keep industrial hemp clearly separate from cannabinoid products in the final 2026 Farm Bill, including the House bill’s industrial hemp provisions.
  2. Support a path to commodity status for industrial hemp, starting with marketing loan eligibility, a permanent crop insurance policy, and processing grants and loan guarantees.
  3. Ask USDA to keep collecting detailed hemp price and acreage data.

[One or two sentences about you: your farm, business, project or why this matters to you.]

Thank you,
[Name]
[Street address, city, ZIP]
[Phone or email]

Your checklist

  • Find my House member and two senators
  • Check whether any of them sit on an Agriculture Committee
  • Send the letter, then call the office
  • Set a Federal Register alert for “hemp”
  • If I grow: answer the USDA hemp survey and report acres to FSA
  • Ask my state legislator about a resolution urging Congress
  • Bring a hemp resolution to my county Farm Bureau
  • Join a hemp organization
  • Ask my city or county about Appendix BL
  • Buy or specify one hemp building product
  • Email me what happened

Every call, comment and permit adds up. Tell me what you tried and what you heard back at advancinghemp@gmail.com, and I’ll share what works.
